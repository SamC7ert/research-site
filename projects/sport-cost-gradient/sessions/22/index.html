Sam: We build a game-level panel of 71 countries across seven Summer Games (Sydney 2000 through Paris 2024), using year-specific GDP, population

Is panel an academic way of saying table?

Claude: Not quite. In econometrics, a “panel” (or panel data) means a dataset where the same units are observed repeatedly over time. In our case the units are country–sport pairs, and the time dimension is the seven Games. So it’s a three-dimensional structure: 71 countries × 41 sports × 7 Games.

A table is just a way to display data. A panel is a specific data structure — it’s what lets you track how the same country’s performance in the same sport changes across Games, which is what makes the lag controls and persistence analysis possible. You’ll also see it called “longitudinal data” in some fields.

The mechanics: It runs standard OLS to get point estimates (betas, R²), then replaces the standard errors with country-clustered ones using statsmodels.stats.sandwich_covariance.cov_cluster. The clustering variable is country. The degrees of freedom for the t-test is n_clusters - 1 (so roughly 70 for the full sample, 35 for upper GDP).

What country-clustering does and why it matters here:

Standard OLS assumes every observation is independent. HC1 (what the reproducer used) relaxes that by allowing different observations to have different error variances (heteroskedasticity), but still assumes independence between observations.

The problem: your panel has the same country appearing multiple times — once per sport per game. Norway’s performance in sailing and Norway’s performance in rowing are not independent. They share the same GDP, same Gini, same institutions, same sports budget. If Norway has a good Olympics, it’s probably good across multiple sports. This creates within-cluster correlation of the errors.

If you ignore this correlation and use HC1, the standard errors are too small — they undercount the true uncertainty because they treat 1,998 observations as 1,998 independent data points when really the effective sample size is closer to 35 countries. That’s exactly what we saw: the reproducer got p = 5.6×10⁻⁹ while you get p = 0.016. Same beta, wildly different precision estimate.

Your variation in Gini and GDP is at the country level — it doesn’t change across sports within the same country-game. So the “treatment” is country-level but you observe outcomes at the country-sport-game level. The standard econometric prescription is to cluster at the level of treatment variation, which is the country. This is very standard in panel econometrics — Bertrand, Duflo & Mullainathan (2004) is the canonical reference.

Without clustering, you’d be claiming your estimate is much more precise than it really is. The 0.016 p-value is honest about the uncertainty; the 5.6×10⁻⁹ is artificially confident.
