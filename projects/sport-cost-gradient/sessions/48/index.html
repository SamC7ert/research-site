Session 48: US Cost Mismeasurement and NCAA Three-Tier Gradient

Summary#

Investigated how US club fees map onto the paper’s cost components (Equipment, Travel, Training_access). Discovered US swimming travel is 2.5–4.8× German travel (€1,400–2,750 vs €567), and that the German-benchmarked cost variable systematically underprices US Swimming (€944 vs real €3,500–7,000) and Artistic Gymnastics (€281 vs $3,500–9,000).

Decomposed the NCAA influence on the regression coefficient sport by sport: Swimming dominates, then Athletics Track, then Artistic Gymnastics. Built a modified Table 7 revealing a three-tier OEP gradient: combat/street sports (~55), athletics (~66), swimming/gymnastics (~83) — tracking the degree of school-system subsidy in each pathway.

Established two distinct exclusion arguments: cost mismeasurement (Swimming, Gymnastics) and omitted variable/NCAA institutional filter (all NCAA sports). Flagged line 209 of the paper draft as incorrect — the attenuation-toward-zero claim is wrong because the mismeasurement is non-classical.

Version: v1.10

Claude: Got it — primary spec uses Equipment + Training_access, Travel is the third component excluded from main runs. The US clubFee ≈ our training_access + travel since clubs bundle tournament travel, coach stipends, facility into one invoice.

Sam: Now you don’t get it. There is a sportcost supplement in paper folder, read that.

Claude: Got it now — primary is Equipment + Travel; training_access is researched for all 47 but deliberately excluded (§3.6). I had it backwards.

So the decomposition question is a validation check, not a spec change. External “club fee” numbers are ambiguous — they can bundle training_access with travel. For a given country where we only have a bundled clubFee figure, the right model-side comparator is Germany’s travel + training_access sum. Then the question becomes: within that bundled number, what share is travel vs training_access? If mostly travel, our primary spec (Eq + Travel) is already capturing the bulk of it.

Sam: I am just debating the framing with myself. How much of the club fee for a high schooler is travel and how much is coaching and swim facility access?

Claude: For US club swimming (USA Swimming year-round, not the high school season team), the typical split: the headline “club fee” / annual dues ($2-4k) mostly covers coach salaries + pool lane rental + admin + USA Swimming registration. That’s training_access in our terminology. Travel (meet entry fees, hotel/fuel for away meets) is usually billed separately per meet, not inside the annual dues.

So for a US club swimmer, the big headline number is predominantly training_access. Travel adds on top. If you’re trying to explain why US club costs look so much higher than Germany, the gap is almost entirely training_access (paid coaches + commercial-rate pool time vs. volunteer coach + subsidised Kommune pool), not travel.

Sam: But what we have in our mainline numbers is travel. So if US travel is equal to German travel we are doing the mainline correct, but there is something outside the mainline we can add as an explanation.

Claude: Yes — that framing holds up. Mainline (Eq + Travel) is defensible as a cross-country comparator precisely because travel is structurally similar: it’s a function of competition geography and calendar density. What varies massively is training_access — because that reflects institutional arrangements (German Kommune + volunteer Verein model vs. US market-rate coach + commercial pool time). That’s a country-level institutional fact, not a sport-level cost difference. Keeping it outside the mainline means the within-country sport ranking isn’t contaminated by an institutional variable that loads on country, not sport.

One thing to sanity-check: is US travel actually ≈ German travel at the youth competitive level, or does US geography push it higher?

Part 4 — School vs Club Pathways and Exclusion Logic#

Sam: So what is the high school cost for travel and training_access for these sports?

Claude: The structural difference is stark:

Track & Field has a fully functioning school pathway — coaching, facility, and competition travel essentially free. A kid from any income bracket can run track in high school at competitive national level. That’s why Track athletes show 30% lower-class.

Swimming has a school pathway but it’s a 3-month season — not enough for competitive development. Real competitive swimmers train club year-round. Family must fund ~$4-8k/yr.

Artistic Gymnastics has no meaningful school pathway. Down 75% since 1977, ~1,500 schools nationally. It’s 100% private club, 100% household-funded, $3.5-9k/yr. That’s why it shows 0% lower-class.

The OEP gradient across NCAA sports tracks the degree to which the school system subsidizes the competitive pathway.

Sam: Athletics track and field, are there private clubs as well?

Claude: Yes — community/volunteer clubs (majority, $0-300/yr, equivalent to German Verein) and elite private training groups ($100-200/month, concentrated in sprint/field events). But the key difference from swimming: the free pathway (school + community club) is fully competitive through to NCAA scholarship level in Track & Field. In swimming, college coaches recruit from club times, not HS times.

Sam: The reason I ask — it is valid to take out swimming and artistic gymnastics, because cost is wrong, but that is not the case with athletics.

Claude: Right. The logic for dropping observations has to be methodological:

Swimming: German-benchmarked cost (€944) is demonstrably wrong for US. Families pay €3,500-7,000. Cost variable is mismeasured. Legitimate to drop or re-price.

Artistic Gymnastics: same — no school pathway, 100% private club at $3-9k/yr, but our cost variable is €281. Massive mismeasurement.

Athletics Track & Field: German cost benchmark (€650-920) is actually reasonable for the US too, because the competitive pathway runs through the school system — just like the German Verein. The cost variable is approximately right. Dropping US Track would be cherry-picking.

Sam: So fair to exclude athletics as well, as the college institutional filter is not modelled?

Claude: It’s a different argument. Swimming + Gymnastics: cost variable is mismeasured — straightforward data-quality exclusion. Athletics: cost is approximately correct, but there’s an omitted variable — the NCAA scholarship incentive creates selection our model doesn’t capture.

Both are legitimate, but if you use the omitted-variable logic for Athletics, it applies to all US NCAA sports. At that point you’re at “drop all US NCAA” (β = −0.29, p = 0.041).

The combined version is cleaner for the paper: “US NCAA sports are excluded because the college athletic scholarship system creates a parallel selection mechanism operating independently of household sport cost. Additionally, for several NCAA sports (swimming, artistic gymnastics), the German-benchmarked cost variable substantially underestimates US household burden.”

Sam: I don’t have time now for the exact framing. But I think we have the concept ready now, and I think the story only becomes more interesting and convincing. The affordability filtering gets documented beyond all doubt.

Claude: Agreed. The pieces are in place:

  1. Cost mismeasurement for Swimming/Gymnastics documented with primary-source US data
  2. NCAA institutional filter visible in Table 7 — three-tier OEP gradient (combat 55 → athletics 66 → swim/gym 83)
  3. The regression holds — dropping contaminated observations tightens SE and improves p-values
  4. The story deepens rather than weakens

Part 5 — Line 209 Attenuation Claim#

Sam: Regarding one, that also indirectly proves affordability filtering as the cost mismeasurement acts as theory shows—

Claude: Yes — when we use the German-benchmarked cost (€944) for US swimming, we’re underpricing a sport whose athletes are overwhelmingly upper-class (OEP 83). The fact that correcting the mismeasurement strengthens the result is itself evidence that the theory is correct.

Sam: Partially right — the mismeasurement gives us a too strong beta but a weaker p. Removing the mispriced lines weakens the gradient but strengthens the p.

Claude: Correct. The underpriced US swimming observations are “cheap sport, rich athletes, strong Olympic performance.” The model interprets that as supporting the inequality-cost interaction — inflating β. But it’s noisy because the cost is wrong, so SE blows up and p weakens.

Drop them: β shrinks from −0.94 to −0.64 (weaker gradient) but SE tightens from 0.38 to 0.23 and p improves from 0.019 to 0.007. The mismeasured observations were adding leverage in the right direction but with so much noise they were hurting precision.

The true affordability gradient is more moderate but more precisely estimated. The mismeasurement was flattering the effect size while undermining statistical confidence.

Sam: There is one statement in the paper which is now wrong, that is relating to using only German costs underestimates the real effect in discussing data. That is wrong.

Claude: Line 209 of paper_v1.10.2_draft.md: “to the extent that the true cost ranking in non-European countries differs from the German benchmark, the coefficient is attenuated toward zero. The gradient’s survival despite a single Central European benchmark makes the estimate conservative.”

That claim is wrong. The mismeasurement is non-classical — systematically wrong in one direction for US sports where institutional costs far exceed the German benchmark. Systematic mismeasurement can bias in either direction. We just showed it inflates β while degrading precision — the opposite of attenuation.

Sam: Yes. [Flag it for fix in next draft.]
