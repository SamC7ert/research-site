    • Gumbel noise distribution: Theoretically motivated by Extreme Value Theory — the deviation of realised maximum from expected maximum follows Gumbel (right-skewed: freak talents possible, downside limited)

    • Euler-Mascheroni centering: Gumbel draws centered by subtracting γ≈0.5772 so E[noise]=0.

Claude: OK. Here’s how the affordability filter works:

For each country-sport pair, it computes a minimum household income to participate: min_income = S + R × equip_cost. S is subsistence (a base cost of living), R is cost_ratio (how equipment cost scales to income need). So for sailing at €4000 equipment with S=5000, R=8: min_income = 5000 + 8×4000 = €37,000.

Then it models household income as lognormal with parameters derived from the country’s GDP and Gini: sigma = gini/100, mu = ln(GDP) - sigma². The fraction of households that can afford the sport is the survival function: 1 - Φ(z) where z = (ln(min_income) - mu) / sigma.

The issue is that this filter is a smooth CDF — the fraction changes gradually as you move up the cost scale. The Gini enters through sigma (the dispersion parameter). Higher Gini means wider spread, which simultaneously puts more households below the threshold AND more above it. For sports near the median income, these effects partially cancel. The net effect is negative (more exclusion in unequal countries for expensive sports), but it’s muted by the offsetting tails.

This is why the gradient maxes out around −0.30. The lognormal is too symmetric in log-space. In reality, income distributions have a Pareto right tail — the rich are much richer than lognormal predicts. This asymmetry means high-Gini countries have a thin group of very wealthy households who can fund any sport, while the mass of households is more concentrated at lower incomes. That would sharpen the exclusion effect for mid-range sports without fully compensating at the top.

Three structural changes that could help for v1.01:

First, replace lognormal with a Singh-Maddala or double-Pareto-lognormal distribution. These have the right shape — lognormal body with Pareto tail — and are standard in the income distribution literature. The Gini still parameterises them, but the exclusion effect is stronger because the right tail is thinner relative to the body.

Second, the threshold S + R × equip_cost is linear in equipment cost. A convex function (cost enters with an exponent > 1) would steepen the gradient because the income requirement accelerates for expensive sports.

Third, the survival function is a hard CDF — you’re either above the threshold or not. A softer version with a participation probability that declines gradually around the threshold might actually strengthen the gradient, because it would make the transition zone wider and more sensitive to distributional shape.

Of these, the income distribution change is most principled — it’s well-established that real incomes follow Pareto tails, not lognormal tails. And it directly addresses the mechanism: the Gini gradient is about how income distribution shape interacts with cost barriers.

Sam: Dagum?

Claude: The Dagum distribution (also called Dagum Type I or Burr III) was introduced by Camilo Dagum in 1977 specifically for income distributions. It has three parameters (a, b, p) and a closed-form CDF:

$$F(x) = (1 + (x/b)^{-a})^{-p}$$

Key properties for us:

It has a Pareto upper tail — the right tail decays as a power law, not exponentially like the lognormal. This means it predicts more very wealthy households but a thinner middle class than lognormal at the same Gini.

It has a closed-form Gini expression involving Beta functions of the parameters. So we can back out the shape parameters from a country’s Gini coefficient, just like we currently back out sigma from Gini for the lognormal.

The LIS comparison study found it was the best 3-parameter distribution in 84% of country datasets tested — far ahead of lognormal, Singh-Maddala, and gamma.

Dagum CDF: $F(x) = (1 + (x/b)^{-a})^{-p}$

Mean: $E[X] = b,\frac{\Gamma(1 - 1/a),\Gamma(p + 1/a)}{\Gamma(p)}$

  1. We know GDP (≈ mean income) and Gini for each country
  2. Fix one shape parameter — say p = 1 (reducing to a 2-parameter Dagum, also known as the log-logistic or Burr Type III)
  3. With p fixed, solve the Gini equation for a: $G = \frac{\Gamma(1),\Gamma(2 + 1/a)}{\Gamma(2),\Gamma(1 + 1/a)} - 1 = \frac{\Gamma(2 + 1/a)}{\Gamma(1 + 1/a)} - 1$

    • Dagum distribution: 3-parameter income distribution (a, b, p) with Pareto upper tail, closed-form Gini: G = Γ(p)Γ(2p+1/a)/(Γ(2p)Γ(p+1/a)) − 1. With p=1 (log-logistic): Gini = 1/a, so a = 1/Gini. Mean: E[X] = b × π/(a×sin(π/a))

    • Bidirectional skewness: Rich prefer expensive sports (status), poor prefer cheap sports (save money) — single δ parameter captures both directions naturally
    • Differential attenuation: Deeper lags absorb slow-moving determinants (Pop, Soviet) but not fast-moving ones (GDP, Gini×equip)
    • Forward model / existence proof: Simulation demonstrates mechanism CAN produce observed pattern

Sam: Think through what a wrong form on that function would do to the results?

Claude: Let me think about what the current log-log linear form actually does and what goes wrong.

The current Layer 2 is: quality += beta × ln(GDP_pc) × ln(non_equip_cost)

This is additive in quality space, which means it’s multiplicative in tournament strength (since quality feeds into an exponential-like tournament). The key property: the marginal effect of GDP on quality is beta × ln(non_equip_cost). This is the same for Norway (GDP_pc = $90,000) and for Turkey (GDP_pc = $30,000). A 10% GDP increase gives the same quality boost to both countries in every sport.

But that’s probably wrong. The first $20,000 of GDP per capita matters enormously for building a velodrome — you go from “no velodrome” to “velodrome exists.” The difference between $60,000 and $90,000 matters much less — both countries have the facility, both have coaching systems, the marginal quality gain is small. The ln(GDP) partially handles this (log is concave), but the interaction with cost is still linear.

Here’s what this does to the Gini gradient tradeoff:

When the calibrator wants a stronger Gini×equip gradient, it needs the preference function to differentiate more across the cost spectrum. But the GDP-quality channel is simultaneously pushing points toward rich countries in expensive sports — because ln(GDP) × ln(high_cost) is large. This GDP push is uniform across all rich countries regardless of their inequality. So Norway and the US both get a big quality boost in expensive sports from GDP, even though their Gini coefficients are very different (27 vs 40).

The regression then has to separate: “expensive sport points go to rich countries” (GDP channel) from “expensive sport points go to equal rich countries” (Gini channel). If the GDP channel is too strong and too uniform, it absorbs variation that should belong to the Gini channel. The regression sees that GDP already explains most of the cost-related variation, leaving less residual for Gini×equip to pick up.

If the GDP-facility effect were more concave — flattening out at high GDP — then above some GDP threshold, the facility advantage would be roughly equal for all rich countries. The remaining differentiation in expensive sports among rich countries would have to come from the Gini channel. The regression would then attribute more to Gini×equip because GDP can’t explain it.

So a too-linear (not concave enough) GDP-facility interaction steals signal from the Gini channel in exactly the parameter region that matters most — upper-income countries in expensive sports. That’s the specification the empirical coefficient of −0.97 comes from.

The fix would be making the GDP-facility channel more concave — perhaps a saturation form where the facility quality approaches a ceiling. Something like beta × [1 - exp(-kappa × GDP_pc / facility_cost)] — at low GDP relative to facility cost, quality rises steeply; at high GDP relative to facility cost, it saturates. Among rich countries, they’d all be near the ceiling for most sports, and the remaining variation would have to come from the affordability (Gini) channel.
