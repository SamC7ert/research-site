Sam: Tell me more about Heckman

Claude: The Heckman selection model addresses a specific problem: when the sample you observe is not random, estimates from that sample may be biased.

The classic example is estimating wages for women. You only observe wages for women who choose to work. If the women who work are systematically different from those who don’t (e.g., more educated, more motivated), your wage estimates are biased because you’re missing the non-workers.

Heckman’s solution is a two-stage procedure. Stage 1 is a probit model that estimates the probability of being in the sample at all (does the woman work? does the country score points in this sport?). Stage 2 is the outcome equation (what’s her wage? how many points does the country score?), but it includes a correction term — the inverse Mills ratio — derived from Stage 1. This correction term accounts for the fact that the sample isn’t random.

The catch: for this to work properly, you need an exclusion restriction — a variable that predicts selection (participation) but doesn’t directly affect the outcome (performance conditional on participation). Without it, the model is identified only through functional form assumptions, which is weak.

In your paper’s context: Stage 1 would model whether a country scores any points in a sport. Stage 2 would model how many points, conditional on scoring. The exclusion restriction would need to be something that predicts whether a country participates in a sport but doesn’t affect how well they do given that they participate. Population is the obvious candidate — bigger countries enter more sports — but population also affects performance (more people = larger talent pool), so it’s a poor exclusion restriction.

Why I think the existing approach is sufficient for your paper:

The Heckman concern is that excluding zeros biases the gradient estimate. But bias from selection goes in a specific direction here. If inequality causes non-participation in expensive sports (which it should, under your mechanism), then excluding those zeros understates the effect — the countries most hurt by inequality in expensive sports aren’t even in the sample. Your estimate is conservative.

Your existing robustness checks already address this from three angles: including zeros (7.2, attenuates but preserves sign), Tobit (7.3, handles censoring), and now the 5% vs 32% participation argument which shows the zeros follow the pattern the mechanism predicts.
