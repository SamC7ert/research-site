Session 44: NCAA Decomposition and Simpson's Paradox

Summary#

This session filled a placeholder in the paper by computing the US empirical-vs-simulated gap by sport, broken down by NCAA status. The result overturned the original framing: the data does not show a clean “US overperforms in cheap sports” pattern. Instead, the US overperforms the simulation in one sport (swimming, +366 points) and underperforms across most non-NCAA sports (-645 aggregate). The NCAA aggregate is almost exactly matched (-14).

A Simpson’s Paradox was discovered: within each NCAA/non-NCAA group, the slope of US points vs equipment cost is positive or flat, but pooling across groups produces a negative slope. The between-group difference (NCAA sports are cheaper on average and the US dominates them) creates the appearance of a cost gradient that does not exist within groups.

Three bugs were also diagnosed in the Stage 3 reproducer’s simulation script: facility cost in millions instead of EUR for Layer 2, raw lag instead of ln(1+lag), and missing lag2. The simulation methodology document was updated to prevent recurrence.

Version: v1.06

Claude: The regression is:

When we estimate beta on all countries we get -0.48 (sim) vs -0.68 (emp). When we remove the US from both, we get -0.25 vs -0.22. The gap shrinks from 0.20 to 0.03.

But we’re removing the US from both the simulated and empirical panels. If the simulation were a perfect replica of reality, removing the US would change both coefficients by the same amount and the gap would stay constant. The gap shrinks because the US contributes differently to the empirical vs simulated gradient.

Your idea about residuals gets at this directly. If we compute predicted points from the regression without the Gini x equip term, then the residual is the part of points that the Gini x equip interaction needs to explain. If the US residual pattern differs between empirical and simulated data, that’s where the gap lives.

Specifically: in the empirical data, the US residuals should show a pattern where cheap-sport residuals are high (US overperforms after controlling for GDP, population, etc.) and expensive-sport residuals are low. That pattern IS the gradient for the US. In the simulated data, the same pattern should exist but weaker — and the difference between the two residual patterns is what causes the 0.20 gap.

Sam: My non-statistical brain has trouble with slopes -18 combined with +16 gives -70.

Claude: The slopes aren’t additive — they’re weighted by the data points. Swimming (1200 empirical at EUR 800) is in the NCAA set. When it’s in the combined pool, it pulls the line steeply negative because it’s such a massive outlier at mid-cost.

Sam: Still not getting it. I understand the weighing, but intuitively when you add -18 with high weight with +16 with low weight you should get -17 or -15, but something lower than -18.

Claude: You’re right, and my “it’s about leverage” explanation is wrong. If you combine two groups where one has slope -18 and the other +16, the combined slope should be between them, not more extreme.

The combined slope is -70 because the NCAA and non-NCAA groups don’t just differ in slope — they differ in level. The NCAA sports have enormous point values (Swimming 1200, Track 803, Gymnastics 322) concentrated at ln(equip) 5.7-6.9. The non-NCAA sports have small values (2-102) spread across ln(equip) 5.3-8.3.

When you combine them, the regression sees: massive points at ln(equip) 6-7 (all NCAA), small points at ln(equip) 7.5-8.3 (all non-NCAA). The drop from 1200 to 66 as you move from ln(6.68) to ln(8.29) creates a steep negative slope — not because either group slopes downward, but because the high-point group sits at lower cost than the low-point group.

It’s Simpson’s paradox. The within-group slopes are -18 and +16. The combined slope is -70 because the groups are separated on both axes — NCAA sports are both cheaper and higher-scoring.

Sam: I understand now. So it is true then that it is the college sports that create the extra gradient.

Claude: Yes, exactly. It’s Simpson’s paradox. The college sports cluster at cheap-to-mid cost AND have high point totals (swimming 1200, track 803). The non-college sports spread across the full cost range with much lower totals. When you combine them, the regression sees “massive points at mid-cost, small points at high-cost” and fits a steep negative line — even though neither group individually has that slope.

The college system doesn’t create the gradient through a cost mechanism. It creates it through a composition effect: the US invested institutionally in specific cheap-to-mid individual sports (swimming above all), producing outsized performance there, while performing normally in expensive non-college sports. That asymmetric investment pattern, combined with high inequality, IS the gradient.

This is the paper story: the US contributes 0.46 to the empirical gradient not because US households can’t afford expensive sports (the affordability mechanism), but because the college system concentrated talent into specific mid-range sports. The simulation captures the affordability part (-0.25 excl USA matches empirical -0.22), but can’t capture the institutional concentration. The 0.23 gap is the college effect.
