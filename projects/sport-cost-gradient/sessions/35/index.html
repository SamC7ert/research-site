Session 35: v1.03 Simulation, Outlier Analysis, Mechanism Gap

Summary#

The v1.03 simulation was run with the final calibrated parameters. Detailed outlier analysis: Turkey, Germany, and Bahrain drive most of the gap between simulated and empirical gradients on the simulation side. The simulation misallocates these countries’ athletes — placing Turkish athletes into expensive sports where Turkey scores zero empirically, and inflating German combat sport points far beyond observed performance.

Removing the top five outlier countries from both regressions collapses the gap from 0.40 to 0.08 — the affordability mechanism explains most of the residual signal, but the absolute magnitudes are small. The paper conclusion was revised: the cost gradient is a real empirical regularity driven by concentrated institutional specialization, not by household cost barriers operating broadly.

The five countries that explain 82% of the sim-empirical gap have specific institutional explanations. Three (Turkey, Germany, Bahrain) are sport-culture misallocations where the simulation assigns athletes to sports those countries don’t historically compete in — these don’t affect the empirical gradient at all. Two (USA, Russia) drive the empirical gradient through well-documented institutional specialization. The affordability channel exists in theory but is too weak to be the primary explanation.

SECTION 8.1 — Two channels (lines 226-231)

Currently presents the talent pool threshold as the explanation: “the effective talent pool is the number of children in families with discretionary income above c.”

Needs tempering. The mechanism is theoretically sound, and the simulation confirms it produces a gradient in the correct direction. But it is very weak — too weak to explain the empirical pattern. The empirical gradient is better characterized as an institutional phenomenon: USA’s college sports system channels investment into expensive individual sports, Russia’s Soviet-era system built depth in cheap combat sports. These institutional histories happen to correlate with the countries’ Gini coefficients.

SECTION 8.3 — Scope conditions and limitations (lines 239-248)

Currently buries the concentration issue in one sentence about the simulation. Needs major expansion. The concentration of the gradient in USA+Russia is a first-order scope condition. In the ind upper spec, removing two countries collapses the coefficient by 80%. In the ind ALL spec, it’s more robust. This is not a limitation to apologize for — it’s a finding that shapes interpretation. The gradient is real but narrow.

SECTION 8.5 — Implications for the literature (lines 260-262)

Currently: “The sport-cost gradient subsumes the institutional channel they identified at the aggregate level.”

This is now too strong. The gradient doesn’t subsume the institutional channel — the institutional channel is probably the main driver. Geloso and Kufenko’s institutional hypothesis may be closer to correct than the household affordability refinement this paper originally proposed. Reframe: this paper’s cost classification provides the empirical tool to identify the gradient, but the mechanism appears institutional rather than household-driven.

SECTION 8.6 — Identification and potential confounders (lines 264-274)

Add a new concern — country concentration. The upper-half gradient depends substantially on USA and Russia. While it survives their removal (p=0.03), and is more robust across all GDP levels (p<0.001), the institutional explanation is at least as plausible as affordability. The Mundlak result (Section 7.6) still holds — the gradient identifies off cross-sport variation within countries — but within-country variation in the USA could reflect the college sports system rather than household income barriers.

SECTION 9 — Conclusion (lines 279-285)

Currently opens: “The cost of a sport determines whether a country’s inequality helps or hinders its Olympic athletes.”

Too strong. Rewrite: The Gini × equipment cost gradient is a robust empirical pattern. But it is concentrated in countries where institutional explanations are readily available, and the affordability mechanism that would provide a causal interpretation is very weak in simulation testing. The gradient documents a real pattern; the mechanism remains open. Future research should focus on disentangling institutional specialization from household affordability — possibly through within-country variation in sport funding, or natural experiments where countries change their sport investment strategies.
