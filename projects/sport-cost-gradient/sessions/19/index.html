Claude: Here’s what Rewilak (2021) does and why it matters for your work:

The Mundlak correction is a way to handle unobserved country-specific effects in a Tobit model (which you need because medal counts are left-censored at zero). Standard random effects assumes the unobserved country effect is uncorrelated with the regressors — clearly wrong here, since countries with high GDP also have high unobserved “sporting culture.” The Mundlak transform adds the time-mean of each regressor as additional controls. This absorbs the between-country variation, so what’s left is essentially within-country variation over time — similar to fixed effects but compatible with Tobit.

What he tested: The standard Bernard & Busse variables — GDP per capita, population, host effect, plus likely political system, climate, etc. Period 1996-2016.

What survived: Only population and host effect. GDP per capita — the variable that’s been “significant” in every cross-sectional study since Bernard & Busse (2004) — drops to insignificant. This means the GDP-medals relationship is entirely between-country (rich countries win more) with no within-country signal (getting richer doesn’t make you win more).

What this means for you: You mentioned you ran some Mundlak controls as well. If your Gini×cost interaction also weakens or disappears with Mundlak, that’s consistent with Rewilak and with your persistence finding. The cross-sectional Gini gradient is real but it’s measuring something time-invariant about countries — their historical sporting specialization — not a contemporaneous affordability mechanism.

This is actually a clean narrative: your simulation shows why the Mundlak correction kills these variables. The affordability channel saturates among rich countries, and what looks like a Gini effect is really persistence in country-sport specialization patterns.
