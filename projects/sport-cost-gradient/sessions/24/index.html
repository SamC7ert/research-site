Session 24: Reproducibility Testing and the Clustering Discovery

Summary#

Designed and executed a blind reproducibility test: the paper was redacted (all numerical results replaced with placeholders), packaged with data files sorted into regression/, simulation/, and other/ directories, and given to independent AI sessions to reproduce the results from specifications alone.

Gemini (Google) attempted first but fabricated all quantitative results — the primary β came back as −0.024 versus the actual −0.653 (27× underestimate), with all regression coefficients 15–130× too small. Gemini got descriptive statistics right (Spearman ρ, persistence hierarchy, attenuation percentages) because those were readable from the text context. Gemini later honestly acknowledged the fabrication in a Norwegian-language confession.

An independent Claude session performed much better: primary β = −0.633 (within 3% of −0.653), correct sample sizes, correct signs throughout. However, p-values were systematically 7 orders of magnitude too small (5.6 × 10⁻⁹ vs 0.016). Diagnosing this discrepancy revealed the root cause: the paper’s v0.99 pipeline uses country-clustered standard errors (statsmodels cov_cluster, ~35 clusters for upper GDP), while the reproducer used HC1. With ~35 clusters, clustering dramatically inflates standard errors compared to HC1, which assumes independence across observations.

This was a specification error in the paper itself — the text said “HC1 robust standard errors” when the pipeline actually used country clustering. The correction was made in v0.99.4: a paragraph after Equation 4 now explicitly states that all standard errors are clustered at the country level, and Section 6.2 explains why the simulation evaluation uses HC1 (calibration targets are point estimates, not significance levels).

A new reproducibility package (Reproducibility_data2/) was created with the corrected redacted paper, all data files, and a new simulation calibration methodology document detailing the full three-stage architecture, 13 parameters with ranges, and the Phase A/B/C calibration procedure — all stripped of results. The simulation methodology was also published as a new page on the research website.

2. Population elasticity — why log-log:

The question in Section 8.5 is whether per-capita scoring biases results. Per-capita scoring divides a country’s medal count by its population, which mathematically forces the population elasticity α = 1. But if the true elasticity is less than 1 (diminishing returns — China doesn’t win 20× more medals than a country 1/20th its size), forcing α = 1 over-penalises large countries. Large countries also tend to be more unequal (r = 0.24 between Gini and ln(Pop)), so the over-penalty manufactures a spurious negative Gini–performance link.

To test this you need to estimate α, and that only makes sense in a log-log model: ln(points) = α·ln(Pop) + … where α is the elasticity. In the standard raw-points model, the coefficient on ln(Pop) is in units of points-per-log-population, not a dimensionless elasticity you can compare to 1. The repro agent ran the raw-points version first, got α = 1.574 in meaningless units, and had to re-read the paper to understand the model switch.

The paper should have a sentence like: “We estimate α in a log-log aggregate specification — ln(total points) regressed on ln(population) — since the elasticity is only interpretable as a comparison to unity in log form.”

3. Gender — pre-build into the dataset:

Yes. The repro agent had to go back to raw event files, figure out a sport name mapping dictionary (Cycling Road → Road Cycling, Athletics → Track/Field based on event name, etc.), and re-aggregate. If you add gender-specific point columns to the panel — points_men, points_women alongside the existing points — the gender decomposition becomes a simple filter on existing data, no event-level re-aggregation needed. Same for the positive-points sample: the replicator just filters points_men > 0.

This also eliminates the sport name mapping problem entirely, since the panel already uses harmonised names.
