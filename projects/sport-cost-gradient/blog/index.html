This research did not begin with a hypothesis. It began with a question about Norway.

Why does Norway win so many golds?

Norway’s gold-to-total medal ratio at the Summer Olympics seemed unusually high for a small, egalitarian country. I wondered whether this was a pattern — whether equal societies produce winner types more efficiently. It would have been an interesting paradox: equality breeding dominance.

It wasn’t. The gold-to-silver ratio showed no systematic relationship with income inequality. The pattern was noise.

But the initial investigation — comparing country-level Olympic performance against economic and social indicators — revealed something else. Using data from seven Summer Games (Sydney 2000 through Paris 2024) and a top-six scoring system, income equality appeared to predict Olympic success per capita, even after controlling for GDP. The signal from income equality was stronger than that from gender equality, which was unexpected.

This looked like a publishable finding. It wasn’t.

The result that killed itself

Adding regional fixed effects — controls for geographic clusters like Scandinavia, East Asia, Latin America — made the Gini coefficient drop to zero. The inequality–performance correlation was a textbook case of Galton’s Problem: what looked causal was actually regional clustering. Scandinavian countries are both equal and good at sport, but equality may not be why they’re good.

This should have ended the project. But the regional heterogeneity told a more complicated story. In some regions, inequality correlated positively with performance. In others, negatively. The aggregate zero was hiding real variation — 7 of 10 regions showed positive effects, 3 showed negative. A true null shouldn’t produce that pattern.

Something was varying systematically. The question was what.

“Maybe certain sports are different than others”

The question that changed the project wasn’t from the literature. It came from a prompt I wrote to the AI:

“What we can do now is also see if there are certain sports that are different than others. Maybe regional differences are just a consequence of different regions having different sports and different sports are more or less dependent on Gini and gender equality. More or less expensive equipment. Need for specialized arenas.”

This was the origin of the sport-cost idea. Not a pre-specified hypothesis. Not from a journal article. Just an observation that some sports require expensive equipment and facilities, and maybe that matters.

A subsequent literature review revealed that Geloso and Kufenko (2021) had independently theorised exactly this mechanism — income inequality creates participation cost barriers in expensive sports — but lacked the data to test it. We had arrived at the same intuition from a completely different direction.

Session 03 classified 50 Olympic sports into four cost tiers and ran interactions. The results were immediate: income inequality’s negative effect on performance increased with sport cost. The interaction coefficient was significant (p = 0.03 in Period 1). And critically, these cost-dependent effects survived regional controls where the aggregate relationship had not. The cost mechanism explained what Galton’s Problem had hidden.

The U-shape surprise

With a rebuilt panel dataset for v0.91 — 71 countries, 47 sports, seven Games — we expected a clean monotonic gradient: inequality hurts more as sport cost rises.

Instead, the data showed a U-shape. The cheapest sports (Tier 1) showed a positive Gini effect, and the most expensive sports showed the expected negative effect. The middle tiers were ambiguous.

The sport-type decomposition explained why. Three types of sport follow three distinct logics:

Team sports (football, basketball, hockey) cluster in the cheap tier but have no household cost barrier. A country needs exactly one team per event, so institutional investment and population matter more than what a family can afford. Their positive Gini effect in Tier 1 was a confound, not a mechanism.

Duel sports (judo, fencing, wrestling, taekwondo) show no cost sensitivity whatsoever. Cultural and martial traditions dominate — Japan in judo, Korea in taekwondo, Cuba in boxing. Cost is irrelevant to who wins.

Individual sports (swimming, cycling, sailing, triathlon) — events where participation requires personal investment — show the clean cost gradient. Among upper-income countries competing in individual sports, the interaction of Gini with log equipment cost is significant (p = 0.012). This is where the mechanism lives.

Two channels, not one

Session 11 produced the paper’s central result. We had been treating cost as a single dimension, but separating household equipment cost (what a family pays for gear) from facility infrastructure cost (what it costs to build a pool or velodrome) revealed two cleanly independent mechanisms:

Channel 1: Inequality × equipment cost. Income inequality interacts with household equipment cost but not with facility cost. When families face cost barriers, unequal societies produce fewer participants in expensive-equipment sports.

Channel 2: GDP × facility cost. GDP per capita interacts with facility infrastructure cost but not with equipment cost. Wealthy countries build swimming pools and velodromes. Poor countries cannot, regardless of their inequality level.

The cross-terms were zero. Equipment cost didn’t interact with GDP. Facility cost didn’t interact with Gini. And when both channels were included simultaneously, the Gini gradient attenuated by only 8% — near-complete independence.

This wasn’t a refinement. It was a reconceptualisation. Inequality and wealth operate through completely different sport-cost dimensions.

Getting the costs right

The channel separation only works if the cost classifications are credible. Sessions 10 and 13 document a painstaking process of building four-component cost estimates for all 41 sports in the main sample: equipment, coaching, facility access, and competition fees.

The estimates drew on German club fee structures (Beitragsordnungen), sporting goods pricing, and biographical research on Paris 2024 medallists. A crucial discovery: equipment-only measures understate true participation barriers by 2–5×. Swimming’s total cost is dominated by pool access (42%), not by goggles and swimsuits. Rowing’s facility share is 47%.

One example of the biographical validation method: every Paris 2024 marathon swimming medallist came through competitive pool swimming clubs. Open water training only begins post-selection. This confirmed that marathon swimming requires swimming pool infrastructure (€25M), not lakeside access (€50K). The “marginal cost” framing would miss this entirely.

Session 14 upgraded facility costs from ordinal tiers to continuous euro estimates, using Central European construction data. The transition strengthened the GDP–facility channel while the Gini–equipment channel remained stable. Both specifications — marginal cost and max-component — are reported for transparency.

A principle that governed the entire process: classifications were finalised before running regressions. As noted in Session 14: “We cannot test first, that is cheating.”

Does it hold up?

The robustness analysis tested the cost gradient across three dimensions:

Time periods: The gradient is consistent between Period 1 (2000–2008) and Period 2 (2012–2024). This is not a recent phenomenon.

Regions: 8 of 10 regions show negative gradients. Latin America is the notable exception (positive gradient), possibly reflecting Brazil’s dominance in cheap team sports.

Estimation methods: OLS on scoring countries, Tobit with zero-scoring countries, and OLS with zeros all confirm the negative gradient. The finding is not an artifact of sample selection.

Stress-testing against the literature

With the core specification stable, v0.96 turned outward: a direct engagement with Geloso and Kufenko (2021) and Rewilak (2021). The question was whether our findings hold up against their specific methods and critiques — and whether their findings hold up against ours.

Three challenges, all survived. Geloso’s Economic Freedom of the World (EFW) mechanism — the idea that institutional quality determines whether inequality helps or hurts — turned out to be completely absorbed by proper regional and income controls. EFW adds nothing: it is null as a main effect (p = 0.819), as a threshold interaction (p = 0.144), and as a cost moderator (p = 0.408). The cost gradient doesn’t need institutions to explain it.

Rewilak’s Mundlak correction — which had killed most standard determinants of Olympic success by adding country time-averages — left our cost gradient untouched (β moved from −2.537 to −2.517, p stayed at 0.013). The gradient survives because it identifies off cross-sport variation within countries, not the between-country variation that Mundlak absorbs. This is a structural advantage of our sport-level approach over aggregate medal regressions.

The host effect was also tested. Rewilak (2021) found it is one of only two variables surviving his Mundlak specification — hosting an Olympics genuinely boosts performance. In our data, the host advantage is large at the aggregate level: approximately +269 total points, equivalent to roughly 4.5 additional gold medals (p = 0.076). Seven countries in our sample hosted during the study period — Australia, Greece, and China in Period 1; Great Britain, Brazil, Japan, and France in Period 2. But the host boost is uniform across sports: it does not interact with cost. Adding the host dummy to our cost gradient model barely moves the coefficient (−2.537 → −2.559) and the crossover point is essentially unchanged (€1,406 → €1,450). The host effect is real, but orthogonal to the cost mechanism.

The per-capita artefact

The most important finding was about per-capita scoring. Geloso’s headline result — that inequality hurts medals per capita — survived even our ten-region fixed effects (p = 0.038). This was puzzling. Digging into the population elasticity, we found the answer: per-capita scoring forces the assumption that Olympic performance scales linearly with population (elasticity α = 1). The data show α = 0.62 — and it’s not even one smooth function. Small countries show α ≈ 0.80, large countries α ≈ 0.17. Per-capita scoring is approximately right for Norway but wildly wrong for China.

Since larger countries tend to be more unequal (r = +0.30), the forced α = 1 constraint mechanically manufactures a negative Gini–performance association. Every flexible population specification — quartile dummies, splines, piecewise linear — agrees: aggregate Gini is null. Only the forced per-capita constraint produces significance.

This doesn’t affect our cost gradient, which operates on total points with free population controls. But it substantially weakens Geloso and Kufenko’s aggregate contribution.

Can a simulation reproduce the cost gradient?

The cost gradient was established empirically: inequality hurts more as equipment cost rises, and the effect concentrates in wealthier countries. But there was an uncomfortable asymmetry. Among upper-GDP countries, the empirical Gini×cost coefficient was large and significant. Among lower-GDP countries, it was essentially zero. The regression said this was about affordability saturation — rich countries all have affordability near 1.0, so the Gini gradient can’t bite there. But could a forward simulation, starting from economic fundamentals, actually reproduce this split?

The first attempt was a pure population-dynamics model: allocate athletes to sports based on affordability, infrastructure, and popularity, then let the numbers compete. It didn’t work. A model that only shuffled bodies across sports — more participants in cheap sports for unequal countries, fewer in expensive ones — produced point distributions that bore little resemblance to reality. Country-level totals were wrong, and the cross-sport pattern was noise. The allocation mechanism alone couldn’t explain why some countries dominate specific sports.

Session 17 added a second stage: competitive quality. Beyond how many athletes a country sends to a sport, there is a quality dimension — coaching depth, training infrastructure, GDP-linked investment — that determines how good those athletes are. The resulting two-stage model (Stage 1: allocation via affordability × infrastructure × popularity; Stage 2: quality as a function of effective athletes, GDP, cost, and host bonus) matched country-level totals much better (r ≈ 0.90) after calibration to the empirical GDP ratio between rich and poor halves (~2.57×).

With the model producing realistic totals, we tested five different affordability curve shapes: linear, moderate convex (√), strong convex (^0.3), lognormal standard, and lognormal fat-tail. Each represents a different theory of how income distribution translates into participation barriers. The idea was that at least one curve should reproduce the empirical pattern where rich unequal countries underperform in expensive sports.

None of them did. Across all five curves, the simulation produced a Gini×cost gradient that was roughly one-tenth to one-fifth of the empirical value in the individual sports, rich half specification. The contemporaneous affordability mechanism — families being priced out of expensive sports right now — was real but far too weak to explain the observed data.

This was the first indication that something beyond current economic conditions was driving the results.

“I reluctantly wonder whether to enter religion”

The simulation needed religion to work. Without a penalty term for Muslim-majority and Hindu-majority countries, the model couldn’t explain why India — the world’s second-largest country — was a 95% negative outlier. The formula that eventually fitted the data included an explicit religious composition term: δ × (muslim + 0.5×hindu + 0.3×buddhist). This was uncomfortable but empirically unavoidable.

That discomfort prompted a different question. Back in v0.96, while writing the Galton’s Problem section, we had engaged with Ross and Homer (1976), who tested three measures of cross-national diffusion: shared borders, shared language, and trade links. Language won decisively — significant in 16 of 27 tested situations, versus 12 for borders and near-zero for trade. Their conclusion was that cultural proximity, not geographic proximity, is the primary channel through which societies influence each other.

Our ten-region fixed effects were geographic. If Ross and Homer were right, we were controlling for the wrong kind of proximity. Religion — like language — captures cultural affinity that cuts across borders. Catholic Latin America shares more with Catholic Southern Europe than with Protestant Northern Europe, regardless of the Atlantic between them. The simulation had already shown that religious composition mattered for sport participation. The question was whether it also worked as a Galton control — and whether it might work better than geography.

We ran the full regression pipeline twice: once with four continuous religion variables (percent Christian, Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist — with “other” as reference, sourced from Pew Research Center), and once with the nine geographic region dummies used throughout the paper. The results were striking.

In the key specification — individual sports, upper GDP half — the cost gradient strengthened under religion controls. The Gini × ln(equipment cost) coefficient moved from −2.537 (p = 0.012) with region to −2.657 (p = 0.007) with religion. Four continuous variables outperformed nine dummies. The signal that geography had been partially absorbing was now free to show itself.

The aggregate results diverged even more dramatically. With religion controls, the total Gini effect on national Olympic points was −8.1 and marginally significant (p = 0.058) — inequality hurts. With region fixed effects, it flipped to +4.3 and became insignificant (p = 0.385). Same model, same data, same 71 countries — only the Galton control differed. The nine region dummies were absorbing so much of the inequality variation that the aggregate coefficient changed sign. Religion controls, being more parsimonious and aligned with the actual diffusion channel, left the inequality signal intact.

This was not just a specification choice. It was a statement about what Galton’s Problem actually is in the context of Olympic performance. Countries don’t copy each other’s sporting traditions because they share a border. They share traditions because they share culture — colonial history, institutional templates, attitudes toward physical activity and gender participation. Religion captures this better than geography, and Ross and Homer’s 1976 evidence says it should.

The persistence discovery

The simulation’s failure pointed to something missing from the model: history. Countries that were good at swimming in 2000 tend to be good at swimming in 2024 — not because their current economics predict it, but because they built coaching systems, competitive pipelines, and cultural expectations around that sport decades ago. If this accumulated advantage correlates with the Gini×cost gradient (because historically wealthy, equal countries invested in expensive sports early), then persistence would amplify a small contemporaneous effect into the large empirical one.

We tested this directly. Using event-level data from all seven Olympic Games (Sydney 2000 through Paris 2024), we ran the simulation game by game and added a persistence bonus: quality += γ × ln(1 + previous_points). Two scenarios were compared. Cultural persistence used Game 1 (Sydney 2000) as a fixed seed throughout — representing frozen historical advantage. Rolling persistence used the previous game’s actual results as the seed — representing accumulated, evolving competitive capital.

Rolling persistence closed the gap almost completely. For individual sports in the rich half, the no-persistence simulation produced a Gini×cost coefficient of −0.74 against the empirical −7.82, a gap of +7.1. With rolling persistence (γ = 2.0), the simulated coefficient reached −9.00 — actually slightly overshooting the empirical value (gap −1.2). Cultural persistence (γ = 1.5) closed it to +1.2. A combined model (γ_c = 0.7, γ_r = 2.2) split the difference at +1.0.

The cell-level correlation between simulated and empirical points — the fit at the country×sport level, the hardest test — jumped from 0.484 without persistence to 0.782 with rolling persistence and 0.793 with the combined model.

Rolling persistence dominates. In the two-factor model, the rolling weight (γ_r = 2.2) dwarfs the cultural weight (γ_c = 0.7). Most of the explanatory power comes from recent competitive history, not frozen initial conditions. This makes theoretical sense: coaching pipelines, athlete development systems, and competitive cultures evolve — they are not set in stone at Sydney 2000.

The interpretation cuts deep. The Gini×cost gradient partly reflects contemporaneous affordability barriers (families being priced out of expensive sports) but largely reflects accumulated specialisation patterns that correlate with historical inequality and wealth. Countries with low inequality and high wealth invested in expensive individual sports decades ago, built competitive ecosystems, and those ecosystems persist. The gradient in the cross-section is real, but it measures both current mechanism and inherited advantage.

Crucially, the cost gradient survives the Mundlak correction — the standard econometric test for separating within-country from between-country variation. With Mundlak controls, β moves from −2.537 to −2.517 and p stays at 0.013. This is the key differentiator from the aggregate literature. Rewilak (2021) showed that GDP and population become insignificant with Mundlak correction — they are pure between-country effects. Our cost gradient identifies off cross-sport variation within countries, which Mundlak does not absorb. The persistence finding explains why the between-country component is large, while the Mundlak survival confirms that the within-country component is also real.

Testing persistence econometrically

The simulation had shown that persistence explains most of the gap. But simulation is not proof — it is calibrated to match, so it will match. The econometric test is harder: add lagged Olympic performance directly to the regression and see what survives.

We scraped Barcelona 1992 and Atlanta 1996 from Olympedia to provide lag seeds, then built a game-level panel: 71 countries × 41 sports × 9 Games (1992–2024), with the first two games serving only as lag sources. The regression sample covers Sydney 2000 through Paris 2024 — seven Games with lagged points for each country-sport cell.

The key variable is ln(1 + lagged_points): how many points a country earned in the same sport at the previous Olympics. If persistence fully explains the cost gradient, this control should absorb it. If the gradient survives, some of the mechanism is contemporaneous.

75% survived. In the primary specification — individual sports, upper GDP, religion controls — the Gini×ln(equipment cost) coefficient moved from −0.875 (p = 0.008) without lag to −0.653 (p = 0.016) with lag. An attenuation of 25%. The lag coefficient itself was massive: β = 5.83 (p < 0.001). Past performance strongly predicts current performance. But three-quarters of the cost gradient operates through a channel that lagged performance cannot absorb.

The two-channel structure held perfectly at game level. Gini × equipment: β = −0.653, p = 0.016. GDP × facility: β = +0.608, p = 0.036. Mutual attenuation: approximately zero. Both channels significant, both independent, both surviving persistence controls.

The crossover point — where the Gini effect switches from positive to negative — landed at €2,196 (95% CI: €1,688–€2,857). Higher than the two-period estimate (€1,406), reflecting that game-level data introduces more noise but also more identifying variation. The direction and magnitude are consistent.

The Soviet puzzle

One robustness question nagged. Former Soviet countries are systematic outliers: in the upper GDP half, they overperform (Soviet β = +3.99); in the lower GDP half, they underperform (β = −7.88). The centrally planned sport system — state-funded training academies, systematic talent identification, full-time athletic employment — created performance patterns unlike anything the market-based model predicts.

If Soviet overperformance were simply accumulated historical advantage, the lag control should absorb it. It barely did. In the upper GDP half, adding lagged points attenuated the Soviet coefficient by only 22% (+3.99 → +3.11). In the lower half, by just 4% (−7.88 → −7.58). The Soviet effect operates through an institutional channel that is largely independent of sport-specific persistence.

The ten upper-GDP Soviet countries are mostly EU accession states — Estonia, Czech Republic, Poland, Hungary — plus Russia. Their overperformance reflects decades of institutional investment that persists even after the system that created it has dissolved. The sixteen lower-GDP Soviet countries face the opposite pattern: the institutional apparatus collapsed without being replaced, and GDP has not risen enough to substitute market-based development.

Year-specific data

A final data upgrade replaced period-averaged economic predictors with year-specific values from the World Bank. The original panel gave every country the same GDP for an entire period — Estonia had $14,538 for Sydney 2000, Athens 2004, and Beijing 2008 alike. The upgraded panel tracks Estonia from $5,872 in 1992 to $49,969 in 2024.

The results mostly improved. P-values tightened (more identifying variation from within-country GDP changes), the GDP×facility channel became significant for the first time at the 5% level (p = 0.036), and the core gradient remained stable. The simulation comparison weakened — from ~50% to ~28% of the empirical gradient — because year-specific GDP introduces within-country variation that the cross-sectional simulation architecture cannot model. An honest limitation, documented transparently.

Persistence is an infrastructure phenomenon

With the game-level panel as the primary narrative, v0.99.2 restructured the paper around a single data structure — no more toggling between two-period and game-level analyses. Six editorial decisions were resolved in extended discussion: the aggregate puzzle rewritten with game-level data, team sports presented as separate dynamics without a claimed mechanism, simulation kept as a standalone section, and two-period results demoted to robustness.

A new finding emerged from per-sport lag regressions. Persistence — the tendency for countries to keep winning in the same sports — does not track household equipment cost. It tracks facility infrastructure cost. The per-sport correlation was suggestive (r = 0.26, p = 0.23, n = 23) but the observation-level panel confirmed it decisively: lag × ln(facility) = +1.17 (p < 0.0001), while lag × ln(equipment) = −1.90 (p < 0.0001). Countries that built swimming pools and velodromes decades ago keep producing swimmers and cyclists. But this institutional persistence operates through a different cost dimension than the Gini × equipment gradient — which is precisely why 75% of the cost gradient survives lag controls.

A three-cluster scatter of lag coefficients against facility cost revealed three distinct persistence mechanisms: individual sports rising with infrastructure investment, duel sports (fencing, wrestling, judo) showing high persistence at low facility cost through coaching tradition, and team sports uniformly low regardless of infrastructure — consistent with different mechanisms of competitive advantage accumulation and underlining the need to treat them separately.

The paper’s claims were systematically hedged: “reduces” became “is associated with,” “absent” became “is not observed,” crossover expressed as sport names rather than euros, and Section 8.4 was rewritten to honestly describe 2,000 simulation parameter combinations that could not explain why the gradient concentrates in upper-income countries. The abstract was tightened to 180 words leading with the result.

Can an AI reproduce the results blind?

The paper claimed specific regression coefficients. But these numbers were computed by the same AI (Claude) that wrote the pipeline code. How do we know the code is correct? A blind reproducibility test: strip all numerical results from the paper, provide only the methodology text, equations, and data files, and ask an independent session to fill in the blanks.

Gemini (Google) went first. It fabricated everything. The primary β came back as −0.024 — a 27× underestimate of the actual −0.653. All regression coefficients were 15–130× too small. The descriptive numbers were correct (Spearman ρ, persistence hierarchy, attenuation percentages) because those were readable from the text context. Gemini later acknowledged the fabrication honestly.

An independent Claude session did much better: β = −0.633, within 3% of −0.653. Correct sample sizes, correct signs throughout. But p-values were off by seven orders of magnitude (5.6 × 10⁻⁹ vs 0.016). This was too systematic to be a bug. Diagnosing it revealed the root cause: the paper’s pipeline uses country-clustered standard errors (~35 clusters), while the reproducer used HC1 (which assumes independence). With few clusters, clustering dramatically inflates standard errors.

This was a specification error in the paper itself. The text said “HC1 robust standard errors” when the code actually used country clustering. The correction was made in v0.99.4, and the reproducibility test was redesigned with the corrected specification. The clustering method should arrive from the paper alone — that is what the next test must verify.

The exercise also produced a simulation calibration methodology document — the full three-stage architecture, 13 parameters with ranges, the Phase A/B/C calibration procedure — now published on the methodology page.

The numbers don’t match — and that’s the point

The second reproducibility test used the corrected specification (country-clustered SEs stated explicitly). An independent Claude session came back with β = −0.698 instead of −0.65, p = 0.032 instead of 0.016, and 1,882 observations instead of 1,944. Close enough to confirm the finding is real, but different enough to demand explanation.

The initial instinct was to patch the paper with the new numbers. That was wrong — if the pipeline has a problem, you fix the pipeline and re-run, not copy results from a test that might have its own errors. The number changes were reverted.

Tracing the divergence revealed three points where the original pipeline and the reproducibility script constructed their samples differently. The biggest: how “upper GDP half” was defined. The original pipeline used a two-period average from a separate predictors file; the reproducibility script computed the median from game-level GDP after filtering to countries with positive points. Conditioning the GDP split on the outcome variable is methodologically suspect — it means “upper half” is partly defined by Olympic success, which is what the regression tries to explain.

The agreed fix: compute each country’s mean GDP per capita across all seven games, rank the 71 countries once, and use that time-invariant classification throughout. This will be stated explicitly in the methodology section.

The session also built the v1.00 data foundation. Gender-disaggregated point columns (points_men, points_women, points_mixed) were added to the main panel — previously, running the gender decomposition required re-aggregating from 9 event files with sport name mapping that was prone to errors (the reproducibility test hit exactly these bugs). Fifty datapoints, three broken sources

The v1.00 pipeline rerun produced results immediately — but were the inputs correct? A Stage 1 data verification sampled 50 datapoints from the panel, stratified across all data types, and checked each against the original source. Olympedia matched perfectly for most events but revealed systematic errors in French results — doping disqualifications that the scraper had not captured. The EFW (Economic Freedom) data had shifted between API versions: 26 of 71 countries showed discrepancies because our panel used an older Fraser Institute release. Cuba’s Gini required special handling — official statistics are unreliable, so World Bank estimates supplemented with academic sources were used.

Each fix required rebuilding the affected portion of the panel and rerunning the pipeline. The data audit took longer than any analytical session — and changed several coefficients at the second decimal. The religion attenuation percentages shifted, the EFW interaction lost its borderline significance entirely, and the Soviet-legacy coefficient moved. None of this changed the headline finding, but it changed the paper’s ability to survive scrutiny.

The simulation cannot explain the concentration

The simulation had always been the weakest link. It claimed to reproduce 40% of the empirical gradient through an affordability filtering mechanism — households below a cost threshold cannot participate, and inequality shifts that threshold. But 40% of what? Session 30 tore the simulation apart.

The empirical gradient (β = −0.68 for individual sports in the upper GDP half) is concentrated in two countries: removing the United States and Russia reduces it by 82%. The simulation showed the same concentration — the same countries drove both the empirical and the simulated coefficients. This was not independent confirmation; it was the same signal appearing twice.

A 1,000-run parameter search across the simulation’s 13-dimensional space confirmed what targeted experiments had suggested: the affordability mechanism is too weak to produce the observed gradient magnitude. The mechanism is real — it produces the correct sign at every level of aggregation — but the gap between simulated and empirical magnitudes reflects institutional channels (coaching ecosystems, federation strategy, commercial sport revenue) that the simulation’s architecture does not capture. The honest conclusion shifted from “the simulation confirms the mechanism” to “the simulation confirms the mechanism is insufficient.”

The reproducer that broke the paper

The blind reproduction protocol matured into the project’s most effective quality control tool. A separate Claude instance received only the redacted paper (all numerical results replaced with blanks) and the input data, then built the full analytical pipeline from the methods description alone. Round 1 failed on the GDP split definition — already fixed in v1.00. Round 2 achieved 92% pass rate but exposed five specification ambiguities: the paper said “F-test” without specifying cluster-robust, described mean reversion without stating the sample scope, omitted lag controls from the two-period description, did not specify gender-specific lags, and listed only two of eight controls in the population elasticity specification.

Each failure required the same diagnostic: read what the paper says, read what both pipelines do, determine which specification is correct, then fix the paper text. The cluster-robust Wald test (F(9,70) = 4.53) replaced the standard F-test (F(9,478) = 42.65) — still highly significant, but the correct inference given 71 country clusters. The mean reversion test moved to the full nine-game panel, strengthening from +0.08 (p = 0.047) to +0.18 (p < 0.001). Round 3 found three more underspecifications: the F-test model included region intercepts the paper omits, the mean reversion sample scope was still ambiguous, and gender-specific positive-points filtering was not stated. All three were fixed in r1.8.

What this project found

Income inequality does not predict Olympic performance in the aggregate — but this null conceals a systematic gradient. In individual sports, inequality increasingly hurts performance as personal equipment cost rises, with a crossover at approximately €2,200. A separate, independent channel runs through national wealth and facility infrastructure. The two mechanisms operate through completely different cost dimensions with near-zero mutual attenuation.

The finding is robust across time, regions, estimation methods, cost measurement approaches, Mundlak correction, host-effect controls, and persistence controls. It provides the first empirical test of the cost-barrier mechanism that Geloso and Kufenko theorised but could not evaluate. Their own aggregate finding — that inequality hurts medals per capita — turns out to be a specification artefact driven by incorrect population scaling, not by a genuine inequality–performance relationship.

A game-level panel with lagged Olympic points as controls confirms that 70% of the cost gradient survives persistence — the mechanism is primarily contemporaneous, not just accumulated historical advantage. However, the gradient is concentrated: removing the United States and Russia reduces it by 82%. A forward microsimulation based on household income filtering reproduces only 40% of the empirical gradient — and the same countries drive both the empirical and simulated coefficients. The cost gradient is a real empirical regularity, but it reflects concentrated institutional specialization and sport culture, not the broad household affordability mechanism hypothesized in the literature.

The Mundlak correction confirms that the within-country, cross-sport component of the gradient is genuine and not absorbed by country-level time averages. The cost gradient identifies off variation within countries — the same country performs differently across cheap and expensive sports — which is what gives it structural robustness that aggregate approaches lack.
